The Engine

Before we depart

A short word about what this journey is, and isn't.

In August 1947, British India was divided into two independent nations — India and Pakistan — in what became the largest mass migration in recorded history. Somewhere between fourteen and sixteen million people crossed a border that had existed, in most places, for less than a week.

This site travels through that history the way so many of its survivors did: by train. Each carriage ahead holds one part of the story — the politics that led here, the timeline of 1947 itself, the migration, and above all, the people who lived it.

The novel Train to Pakistan by Khushwant Singh runs alongside this journey as a companion, not a destination. It gives this site its central image — the train as both a vessel of escape and, too often, of tragedy — but the story this site tells is the Partition's own.

This is a working prototype. Wherever you see the word placeholder, that space is reserved for real testimony, photographs, or recordings to be added with proper consent before public launch.

Carriage One

What was Partition?

The short answer, and why it is never quite short enough.

In one paragraph

On 14–15 August 1947, British India ended almost two centuries of colonial rule and split into two independent dominions: India, with a Hindu-majority population, and Pakistan, created as a homeland for the subcontinent's Muslims and itself divided into two wings a thousand miles apart (West Pakistan and East Pakistan, now Bangladesh). The new international border cut directly through the two provinces with the most religiously mixed populations, Punjab and Bengal, dividing farmland, rivers, cities, and families.

Why it still matters

Partition was not simply a redrawing of a map. It triggered a migration of an estimated 14–16 million people, communal violence that killed anywhere from 200,000 to two million (historians still dispute the number), and the abduction of tens of thousands of women. It shaped two nations' founding identities, and its aftershocks — in family memory, in politics, in the India–Pakistan relationship — continue today.

Key facts

  • Date: Independence and Partition took effect at midnight, 14–15 August 1947.
  • Boundary: Drawn by the Radcliffe Commission, chaired by a British lawyer who had never been to India before his appointment.
  • Two nations: The Dominion of Pakistan and the Dominion of India (both became republics later — Pakistan in 1956, India in 1950).
  • Two wings: Pakistan initially comprised West Pakistan and East Pakistan; the latter became independent Bangladesh in 1971.
  • Basis of division: District-level religious majorities, applied unevenly and under enormous time pressure.

Carriage Three

Causes of Partition

No single cause explains 1947. These forces built on one another over four decades.

Colonial policy

British administrative practices — including separate electorates for Muslims introduced in 1909 — institutionalised religious identity as the primary category of Indian politics, deepening a divide that colonial rule had also long exploited to govern.

The two-nation theory

The Muslim League, led by Muhammad Ali Jinnah, argued that Hindus and Muslims constituted two distinct nations that could not fairly share one state — a position formalised in the 1940 Lahore Resolution.

Failed power-sharing

The 1946 Cabinet Mission Plan attempted to keep India united under a loose federal structure. Its collapse, amid mutual distrust between the Congress and the League, removed the last serious alternative to partition.

Direct Action Day

The August 1946 call for "Direct Action" and the killing that followed in Calcutta convinced many leaders, reluctantly, that a shared future was no longer safe to attempt.

A compressed timeline

Mountbatten's decision to move independence forward by nearly a year gave officials weeks, not months, to plan the transfer of power, security, and population — a compression many historians see as its own cause of the disaster that followed.

An imperfect boundary

The Radcliffe Line was drawn using outdated maps and incomplete 1941 census data, in five weeks, by a commission that could not visit most of the territory it was dividing.

Migration and the refugee crisis

The scale of it

Refugees moved in "foot columns" (kafilas) that could stretch for eight to ten miles and take days to pass a single point, and aboard trains that became so associated with mass killing that they earned the grim nickname "ghost trains" — arriving at their destination with every passenger dead. Both India and Pakistan established emergency ministries and camps to register, feed, and eventually resettle the displaced, a process that in practice took years, and for some, was never fully completed.

Carriage Six

Women and children

Partition bore down on women and children with particular cruelty, and history has only recently begun to reckon with it fully.

Abduction and "recovery"

An estimated 75,000 women were abducted across the new border during and after Partition. The Indian and Pakistani governments later ran a joint "recovery" operation to locate and return abducted women to their original families — a process that was itself often experienced as a second uprooting, since many women had, by then, built new lives, families, or simply no longer wished to return to communities that in some cases would not fully accept them back.

Honour and violence

In several documented cases, women were killed by their own male relatives to prevent their capture or conversion by the "other" community — a practice some survivors and historians have since described as a violence born of the same communal logic driving the wider killing, rather than separate from it.

Children in the crossing

Thousands of children were separated from parents in crowded stations and chaotic convoys. Camps and volunteer organisations — many run by women, including the All India Women's Conference — worked for years afterward tracing missing children through registries, newspaper notices, and word of mouth.

A note on this section

Scholars including Urvashi Butalia (The Other Side of Silence) and Ritu Menon & Kamla Bhasin (Borders and Boundaries) were among the first to centre women's experience of Partition in historical writing, decades after the event. Their work is recommended further reading.

Carriage Seven

Religion and identity

Before 1947, religious identity coexisted, often literally next door, with shared language, food, dress, and daily life.

In villages like the fictional Mano Majra of Train to Pakistan, Sikh and Muslim families had lived side by side for generations, sharing wells, festivals, and labour. Partition asked people to sort centuries of shared, syncretic life into two boxes — Hindu-Sikh and Muslim — almost overnight, and to treat neighbours as strangers, or worse, on that basis alone.

This did not happen everywhere, or evenly. Many individuals sheltered neighbours of another faith at real risk to themselves; entire communities in some regions saw little violence at all. But where fear took hold, it moved fast, often driven less by personal animosity than by rumour, retaliation for violence elsewhere, and the collapse of any authority able to guarantee safety.

"The mob had many heads but no head." — a description echoed across numerous survivor accounts of how ordinary people were swept into collective violence they would not have chosen alone.

Understanding this is essential to understanding Partition honestly: it was not a war between religions so much as a catastrophic failure of political and administrative processes that then expressed itself through the fault line of religious identity.

Carriage Eight

Politics and leaders

Presented neutrally: the people whose decisions shaped 1947, without ranking blame.

Muslim League

Muhammad Ali Jinnah

Led the Muslim League and became Pakistan's first Governor-General. Argued Muslims required a separate state to protect their political and cultural future.

Indian National Congress

Jawaharlal Nehru

Congress leader and India's first Prime Minister, committed to a united, secular India until partition became unavoidable.

Indian National Congress

Mahatma Gandhi

Opposed Partition to the end and spent independence day fasting and working against communal violence in Calcutta rather than celebrating in Delhi.

British Crown

Lord Louis Mountbatten

The last Viceroy of India, tasked with transferring power; accelerated the timetable for independence to August 1947.

Boundary Commission

Sir Cyril Radcliffe

British barrister with no prior experience of India, given five weeks to draw the Punjab and Bengal boundary lines.

Pakistan Movement

Liaquat Ali Khan

Jinnah's deputy and Pakistan's first Prime Minister, central to the Muslim League's negotiating position through 1946–47.

Indian National Congress

Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel

Congress leader and India's first Deputy Prime Minister, who oversaw the integration of princely states into the new Indian union.

Carriage Nine

Culture, art and memory

How Partition has been remembered, painted, written, and preserved in the decades since.

Literature and art

Partition produced one of South Asia's richest bodies of literature — Saadat Hasan Manto's unflinching short stories, Amrita Pritam's poetry and Pinjar, Bhisham Sahni's Tamas, Khushwant Singh's Train to Pakistan, and Bapsi Sidhwa's Cracking India, among many others — because fiction could sometimes carry what official history could not yet hold.

Living memorials

The Partition Museum in Amritsar (opened 2017) and the 1947 Partition Archive, a global oral-history project, are two of the most significant efforts to collect survivor testimony directly, while survivors are still alive to give it.

Carriage Ten

Impact on families

A single border decision reached into households and rearranged them permanently.

Severed extended families

Joint family structures common across pre-Partition Punjab and Bengal were frequently split by the border itself, with siblings, parents, and grandparents ending up as citizens of different countries — sometimes only a few miles, but a closed border, apart.

Names, papers, and property

Families arrived with whatever documents they could carry, if any. Refugee registration became the basis for claims to compensation and resettled property — a bureaucratic process that could take years and rarely restored what had been lost.

Generational memory

Many families who lived through Partition spoke about it rarely, if at all, to their children — a documented pattern of protective silence that later generations, including today's descendants, are now actively working to recover through oral history projects.

Reunions, decades later

Since India and Pakistan eased some travel and, more recently, opened cross-border correspondence via social media, numerous families have found and visited relatives, or the homes their families left, after seventy or more years apart.

Carriage Eleven

Economic and social impact

Numbers, presented the way this journey prefers to present them: as luggage tags, not spreadsheets.

Divided industries

Punjab's canal-irrigated farmland — among the most productive in the subcontinent — was split by a border that ignored irrigation networks, creating water disputes that echo into modern India–Pakistan relations. Bengal's jute industry was similarly divided: most jute-growing land fell to East Pakistan, while most jute mills remained in Calcutta, India — severing raw material from processing capacity overnight.

Carriage Twelve

Media representation

Film, documentary, and print have each shaped public memory of Partition differently. Spin a reel to read more.

How memory gets shaped

Popular cinema in both countries has, at times, leaned toward nationalist framing — casting the "other" side as primarily responsible for violence. More restrained works, including Train to Pakistan itself, tend to distribute moral weight across communities and blame the collapse of order and leadership more than any one group. Newspapers of the period were themselves partisan instruments: many English and vernacular papers in 1947 openly took communal sides, complicating their value as neutral historical sources and reminding modern readers to weigh period reporting carefully.

Carriage Thirteen

Train to Pakistan

The novel that lends this site its central image, examined on its own terms.

Train to Pakistan

Khushwant Singh, 1956

Set almost entirely within the fictional border village of Mano Majra, on the banks of the Sutlej, Train to Pakistan was one of the first major novels in English to confront Partition directly, only nine years after it happened. It refuses easy heroes: violence here is committed and resisted by ordinary people of every community, and the novel's moral centre — the small-time criminal Juggut Singh — is redeemed by love and courage, not ideology.

Rather than dramatising politicians and boundary commissions, the novel stays at ground level, in a single village, and asks what Partition actually required of an ordinary person the moment it arrived at their door.

Mano Majra is a small village where Sikhs and Muslims have lived together for generations, its rhythms set by the trains that pass through daily. When a ghost train arrives carrying only corpses, and a moneylender is murdered the same night, suspicion and fear take hold — testing every relationship the village had taken for granted.

Juggut Singh

"Jugga"

A Sikh badmash (small-time criminal) in love with a Muslim woman, Nooran; his final act of sacrifice is the novel's moral climax.

Nooran

Imam Baksh's daughter

A young Muslim woman whose relationship with Jugga crosses the line Partition is about to draw in blood.

Iqbal Singh

The outside idealist

A city-educated social worker whose political theorising proves nearly useless against the village's real crisis.

Hukum Chand

The magistrate

A weary, morally compromised local official who nonetheless intervenes at a decisive moment.

Imam Baksh

The village weaver

Respected Muslim elder forced, like his neighbours, to leave the only home he has known.

The train itself is the novel's central symbol — normally a sign of connection and modern progress, it becomes, in the "ghost trains," an instrument of atrocity. Water (the Sutlej river, the village well) recurs as a symbol of shared life that violence cannot fully sever. The novel's structure — spending most of its length on ordinary village routine before violence arrives — insists that Partition happened to ordinary life, not around it.
Trains carried refugees to safety and, in the same season, carried the murdered as gruesome cargo. No single image captures Partition's whiplash between hope and horror as precisely — which is why this entire website borrows the train as its structuring idea.
Mano Majra is fictional, but Khushwant Singh drew on his own experience: he practised law in Lahore until 1947 and witnessed Partition violence firsthand before migrating to India. Historians generally regard the novel's depiction of communal breakdown, refugee trains, and administrative helplessness as an accurate, well-observed composite of real events, even though its specific plot is invented.

Carriage Fourteen

Legacy today

Partition is not a closed chapter. It is a living inheritance, still being written.

Institutions of memory

The Partition Museum in Amritsar and the 1947 Partition Archive are racing against time to record survivor testimony while the generation that lived through 1947 is still with us — most survivors are now in their nineties or older.

The border today

The Wagah–Attari border crossing between Lahore and Amritsar remains one of the few official land crossings, its daily flag-lowering ceremony drawing visitors from both countries who gather, separated by a gate, to watch essentially the same ritual performed in mirror image.

Diaspora memory

Partition also shaped South Asian diaspora communities worldwide, many of whom carry family memory of migration a second or third time removed, and who have become active contributors to oral history and archival projects.

Why this still matters to students

Understanding Partition helps explain contemporary India–Pakistan relations, the politics of Kashmir, and broader patterns of how nations are made and unmade — but its most durable lesson may simply be how ordinary people behave, for better and worse, when the ground shifts beneath them without warning.
